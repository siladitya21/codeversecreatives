Week 15: Capstone — Full System Design Interview Simulation

This is the final week — no new concepts, just Weeks 1–14 applied end to end, under the same conditions as a real onsite loop. You'll pick one large, real-world system, run it through the complete requirements-estimation-design-deep dive framework from Week 1, produce a written design doc a reviewer could actually evaluate, and rehearse presenting it out loud against a timer — the skill an interview actually tests, not just the diagram at the end of it.

Module 12 of 12 Week 15 of 15 ~10–15 Hours Capstone Project

By the end of this capstone, you'll be able to

  • Scope and estimate a large, ambiguous system from a one-line prompt
  • Produce a complete design doc with diagrams, estimates and explicit tradeoffs
  • Present and defend that design out loud, under time pressure, the way a real interview does

1. Choosing & Scoping the System

Pick one system, not several — a capstone that shallowly covers three systems demonstrates less than one covered deeply. A good choice is large enough to touch most of this course's modules, but familiar enough that you're not learning the domain and the design process at the same time:

  • A ride-sharing dispatch system — matching riders to nearby drivers, real-time location updates, pricing. Touches geospatial indexing, real-time messaging, and consistency under contention.
  • A video streaming platform — upload, transcode, and serve video at scale. Touches storage tiers, CDN delivery, and asynchronous processing pipelines.
  • A social media news feed — posts, follows, and a personalized, low-latency feed. Touches fan-out strategies, caching, and read/write asymmetry.

Whichever you pick, write a one-paragraph scope statement before anything else — exactly the discipline Week 1 built: what the system does, for whom, and what's explicitly out of scope. "Design a ride-sharing dispatch system: riders request a trip and are matched to the nearest available driver within 5 seconds; payments, driver onboarding and support tooling are out of scope" tells a reader immediately what they're about to evaluate.

2. Requirements & Estimation

Apply Week 1's framework in full, on paper, before sketching a single box:

requirements & estimation — ride-sharing dispatch, example skeleton
Functional:
- A rider can request a trip from location A to location B
- The system matches the rider to the nearest available driver
- Both parties see live location updates until pickup

Non-functional:
- Scale: 5M daily active riders, ~2M trip requests/day
- Latency: match must complete within 5 seconds
- Consistency: a driver must never be matched to two riders
  at once -- this one needs strong consistency, unlike most
  of this course's read-heavy examples
- Availability: brief unavailability during a region's off-peak
  hours is tolerable; unavailability during peak hours is not

Estimation:
  2,000,000 requests/day / 86,400 sec ≈ 23 requests/sec average
  Peak (rush hour concentration) ≈ 23 * 8 ≈ 180 requests/sec
  Each active driver sends a location ping every ~4 seconds:
  500,000 active drivers / 4 sec ≈ 125,000 location writes/sec

That last line is the number that should reshape the whole design: 125,000 writes/ second of location data dwarfs the 180 requests/second of actual trip matching. A design that treats both as equally weighted traffic hasn't understood its own system yet — the location-update pipeline needs its own scaling story (likely a write-optimized store and in-memory geospatial index), separate from the transactional trip-matching path that needs the strong consistency called out above.

3. High-Level Design & Deep Dives

Sketch the minimal architecture per Week 1's diagramming discipline, then pick two or three components to go deep on — the ones your own estimation numbers just flagged as the hard parts:

architecture sketch — ride-sharing dispatch, v1
[Rider App] --> [API Gateway] --> [Trip Service] --> [Matching Service]
                                                            |
[Driver App] --location ping--> [Location Ingest] --> [Geospatial Index]
                                                            ^
                                                Matching Service reads this

Trip Service:   owns trip state, needs strong consistency (Weeks 9-10)
Matching:       queries the geospatial index for nearby available drivers
Location Ingest: write-heavy pipeline, likely backed by a queue (Week 8)
                 to absorb bursts without blocking the driver app

Deep dive candidates naturally fall out of the estimation numbers from Section 2: the geospatial index (how do you efficiently query "which available drivers are within 2km of this rider?" — geohashing or a quadtree, and how it's kept up to date under 125,000 writes/second), the matching algorithm's consistency guarantee (how do you guarantee exactly one rider per driver without a race, using the CAP-theorem vocabulary from Weeks 9–10), and the location-ingest pipeline's use of a queue to decouple the driver app's ping rate from downstream processing (Week 8). Each deep dive should end with an explicit tradeoff, not just a description of how the component works — see Section 4.

4. Tradeoff Analysis & Failure Scenarios

A design without stated tradeoffs reads as if you don't know what you gave up — every real design decision costs something, and naming that cost is what separates a strong answer from a list of technologies:

  • Strong consistency on trip matching costs latency and availability during a partition — worth it here because a double-matched driver is a broken product experience, unlike a stale click count in Week 1's URL shortener example.
  • A queue absorbing location pings costs freshness — a driver's shown position may lag the queue's processing delay by up to a few hundred milliseconds, an acceptable tradeoff against the alternative of location writes blocking on synchronous processing.
  • Geospatial index sharded by region costs cross-region query complexity for a rider near a boundary, in exchange for keeping each shard's write volume manageable.

Then walk through at least one failure scenario end to end, the way an interviewer's follow-up question would: "what happens if the Matching Service goes down for 30 seconds during peak hours?" A strong answer names the concrete user-facing impact (new match requests queue or fail; in-flight trips are unaffected since Trip Service owns that state independently), and the recovery path (requests retry against a healthy replica; no data is lost because location pings were already durably queued in Section 3's design).

"It depends" is a real answer, if you finish the sentence

The strongest design interview answers use "it depends" as an opening, not an ending: "it depends — if writes were rare, I'd favor strong consistency; here, given 125,000 writes/second, I'd favor..." A tradeoff stated without the "and here's what determined my choice for THIS system" half sounds like uncertainty rather than judgment.

5. Writing the Design Doc

Compile Sections 1–4 into a single document a reviewer could read without you in the room — the actual artifact this capstone produces:

  1. Scope — the one-paragraph statement from Section 1.
  2. Requirements & estimation — the functional/non-functional list and the math from Section 2, shown, not just stated.
  3. Architecture diagram — the sketch from Section 3, legible on its own.
  4. Deep dives — each component you went deep on, with its tradeoff explicitly stated.
  5. Failure scenarios — at least one, walked through end to end, from Section 4.
  6. What you'd do differently — an honest note on what you'd reconsider with more time or a different constraint (e.g. global scale instead of single-region).

Then rehearse presenting it out loud, against a 35–40 minute timer, the length of a real system design interview round. Recording yourself and listening back is uncomfortable and disproportionately useful — it's the fastest way to notice you're spending twelve minutes on the diagram and two minutes on tradeoffs, when a real interview rewards roughly the opposite balance.

6. The Capstone Project

Capstone

Design, document and rehearse one large system end to end

Take one system from Section 1's list (or one of your own choosing of comparable scope) fully through Sections 1–5: scoped, estimated, designed, analyzed and written up.

Requirements:

  1. Write the scope statement and full functional/non-functional requirements list before doing any estimation math (Section 1).
  2. Complete the back-of-envelope estimation — average and peak throughput for every distinct traffic pattern in the system (not just one aggregate number), plus a storage estimate (Section 2).
  3. Produce a high-level architecture diagram and go deep on at least two components, each ending in an explicit tradeoff (Section 3).
  4. Walk through at least one realistic failure scenario end to end, naming the user-facing impact and the recovery path (Section 4).
  5. Write the complete design doc covering all six sections from Section 5.
  6. Rehearse presenting the design out loud against a 35-40 minute timer at least twice, ideally recorded.
Find a practice partner if you can

Presenting to an empty room and presenting to someone who can ask a follow-up question you didn't anticipate are very different exercises — the second is what actually simulates an interview. If you can't find a partner, write down three follow-up questions you'd expect an interviewer to ask, and answer them cold before checking your design doc.

7. Final Checklist

Before calling the capstone — and the course — done, confirm each of these honestly:

✓

Were the requirements and estimation written down before the architecture diagram was drawn?

A diagram drawn first and justified afterward tends to describe a generic, memorized architecture rather than one derived from this system's specific numbers. If the estimation step genuinely happened first — the way Section 2's 125,000 writes/second number reshaped the ride-sharing design — the diagram should visibly reflect it, with the highest-traffic path getting the most deliberate design attention.

✓

Does every deep-dive component end with an explicit tradeoff, not just a description of how it works?

Explaining how a geospatial index works is a knowledge check; explaining what you gave up by choosing region-sharding over a single global index is a design decision — the second is what an interview loop is actually scoring. A deep dive that only describes mechanics, with no stated cost, hasn't demonstrated judgment yet.

✓

Was at least one failure scenario walked through with a concrete user-facing impact and recovery path, not just "it would handle failures gracefully"?

"It would handle failures gracefully" is a claim with no evidence behind it. Naming the specific component that fails, what a user actually experiences during that failure, and the specific mechanism that recovers it — the way Section 4's Matching Service outage was traced through to "requests queue or fail, but in-flight trips are unaffected" — is what proves the design was actually reasoned through rather than asserted.

✓

Was the design presented out loud against a timer, not just written down?

A design interview scores real-time communication and time management alongside the design itself — spending twelve minutes on a diagram and two on tradeoffs is a failure mode that's invisible on paper but obvious the moment you rehearse against a clock. If this step was skipped, the capstone has produced a design doc but not yet practiced the actual skill the course exists to build.
