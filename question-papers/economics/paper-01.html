Part I — Question 1

(i) Scarcity is the fundamental economic problem arising because human wants are unlimited while the resources available to satisfy them are limited. (ii) Opportunity cost is the value of the next best alternative given up when a choice is made. (iii) Other things remaining constant, the quantity demanded of a commodity varies inversely with its price. (iv) Elasticity of demand is the degree of responsiveness of quantity demanded to a change in price (or income, or the price of related goods). (v) A PPC shows the maximum possible combinations of two goods an economy can produce with its given resources and technology, assuming full and efficient use. (vi) Any two: what to produce; how to produce; for whom to produce. (vii) A monopoly is a market with a single seller of a product with no close substitutes, giving that seller significant control over price. (viii) A fixed cost does not change with the level of output in the short run, and is incurred even at zero output (e.g., rent). (ix) Public finance is the study of the income and expenditure of the government, including taxation, public expenditure, public debt and budgetary policy. (x) Inflation is a sustained rise in the general price level of goods and services over a period of time.

✓

Question 2 — Demand, Supply & Market Equilibrium

(a) The Law of Demand states that, other factors remaining constant, there is an inverse relationship between the price of a good and the quantity demanded of it — as price falls, quantity demanded rises, and vice versa. Example: if the price of apples falls from ₹200/kg to ₹150/kg, consumers are likely to buy more apples, all else being equal.

(b) A "change in quantity demanded" is a movement along the same demand curve, caused only by a change in the price of the good itself. A "change in demand" is a shift of the entire demand curve, caused by a change in factors other than price — such as income, tastes, or the price of related goods.

(c) The equilibrium price is the price at which quantity demanded exactly equals quantity supplied. It is determined at the point where the demand curve and the supply curve intersect.

✓

Question 3 — Central Problems & the PPC

(a) What to produce: deciding which goods and services to produce, and in what quantities, given limited resources. How to produce: choosing the technique or method of production — e.g., labour-intensive versus capital-intensive. For whom to produce: deciding how the output produced is distributed among the members of society.

(b) A point ON the PPC represents full and efficient utilization of resources. A point INSIDE the curve represents underutilization of resources — production of both goods could increase without giving up any of either. A point OUTSIDE the curve is currently unattainable with the economy's existing resources and technology, though it could become attainable through economic growth, which shifts the PPC outward.

✓

Question 4 — Money & Banking

(a) Money is anything generally accepted as a medium of exchange and a measure of value in a society. Two functions: a medium of exchange (facilitates buying and selling without barter) and a measure/store of value (a standard unit for measuring value, and a way to hold wealth over time).

(b) Any three: a commercial bank accepts deposits and lends to the public, while a central bank does not deal directly with the general public but regulates commercial banks; only the central bank has the sole authority to issue currency notes; the central bank acts as a "lender of last resort" to commercial banks; the central bank formulates monetary policy for the whole economy, while commercial banks operate within that policy framework.

✓

Question 5 — Unemployment in India

(a) Unemployment is a situation where people who are able and willing to work at the prevailing wage rate are unable to find work. Disguised unemployment occurs when more people are engaged in an activity (often agriculture) than are actually needed, so removing some would not reduce total output — their marginal productivity is effectively zero. Seasonal unemployment occurs when people are unemployed during certain months of the year because work is available only in specific seasons, e.g., agricultural labourers during the off-season.

(b) Any two causes: rapid population growth outpacing job creation; slow industrial growth; the seasonal nature of agriculture; a mismatch between workers' skills and job-market needs. One measure: promoting labour-intensive small-scale and cottage industries to generate more employment opportunities (also accept: skill-development/vocational training programs, or encouraging self-employment through government schemes).
