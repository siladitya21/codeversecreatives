English Language — Mock Paper 1

Section A tests writing; Section B tests reading. Attempt the whole paper — composition included — before opening the marking guide, since writing under time pressure is a skill in itself.

Maximum Marks: 80 Time Allowed: 2 Hours Section A: 35 (Writing) Section B: 45 (Reading)
1 General Instructions 2 Section A — Writing 3 Section B — Reading 4 Marking Guide

1. General Instructions

  • Answers to this paper must be written on separate answer sheets.
  • You will not be allowed to write during the first 15 minutes — this time is to be spent reading the question paper and planning your composition.
  • Attempt all four questions.
  • The intended marks for each question are given in brackets [ ].
  • You are reminded of the need for good English and clear presentation throughout.
Writing questions are marked by a guide, not a single "correct" answer

The Composition and Email carry no fixed sample answer — instead, Section 4 gives you the criteria an examiner actually checks (content, organisation, language) so you can self-assess honestly. The Comprehension and Summary, by contrast, do have specific correct answers, since they're based on this paper's own passages.

2. Section A — Writing (35 Marks)

Question 1 [25] — Composition

Write a composition of about 500 words on any one of the following:

  1. Descriptive: "A Journey I Will Never Forget."
  2. Narrative: Write a story that begins with the line: "The letter had been sitting unopened on the table for three days."
  3. Argumentative: "Social media does more harm than good." Do you agree? Give reasons for your view.
  4. Reflective: "The most valuable lesson I learned outside the classroom."

Question 2 [10] — Formal Email

Write a formal email on either of the following, in about 150 words:

  1. Write to the Principal of your school requesting permission to organize an inter-house debate competition. Mention your reason, a proposed date, and the benefits you expect it to bring.
  2. Write to the Editor of a local newspaper about the problem of garbage disposal in your locality, suggesting at least two practical solutions.

3. Section B — Reading (45 Marks)

Question 3 [25] — Comprehension

Read the passage below carefully and answer the questions that follow, in your own words as far as possible.

unseen passage
For decades, people believed that talent was something you were born
with -- you either had "a gift" for music, mathematics or sport, or
you did not. Recent research into skill acquisition tells a more
encouraging story. Anders Ericsson, a psychologist who studied
violinists, chess players and surgeons for over thirty years, found
that the single biggest difference between experts and amateurs was
not natural ability but the amount of deliberate practice each had
accumulated.

Deliberate practice is not the same as simply repeating a task for
years. A taxi driver who has driven the same city for two decades
does not necessarily become a better driver in that time, because
ordinary driving does not push him beyond what he already finds
comfortable. Deliberate practice, by contrast, deliberately targets
weaknesses. It requires a learner to attempt something slightly
beyond their current ability, receive immediate feedback on what
went wrong, and try again with that feedback in mind. A young
violinist who plays the same piece from beginning to end every day
improves far more slowly than one who isolates the four bars she
keeps stumbling over and repeats only those, adjusting her fingering
each time until the mistake disappears.

This idea matters far beyond music and chess. It suggests that the
students who eventually outperform their classmates in mathematics
or a second language are rarely the ones who started with some
innate advantage; they are usually the ones who practiced in this
focused, uncomfortable way for the longest time, and who treated
every mistake as information rather than as proof of a fixed
limitation.

None of this means effort alone guarantees mastery -- a good coach,
a well-structured curriculum and honest feedback all matter
enormously. But it does mean that the old, comforting excuse of
"I'm just not a maths person" rests on much shakier ground than it
once appeared to. The gap between a novice and an expert, more often
than not, is simply a gap in the number of hours spent practicing
the right way.
  1. According to the passage, what did Anders Ericsson study, and for how long? [2]
  2. What, according to Ericsson's research, is the biggest difference between experts and amateurs? [2]
  3. Why does the taxi driver's decades of driving NOT count as deliberate practice? [4]
  4. Explain, in your own words, what deliberate practice requires of a learner. [4]
  5. What does the example of the young violinist illustrate? [3]
  6. According to the passage, what usually explains why some students outperform their classmates? [2]
  7. Does the author claim that effort alone guarantees mastery? Support your answer with a phrase from the passage. [4]
  8. Find a word from the passage (paragraph 1) that means the same as "natural ability." [1]
  9. Find a word from the passage (last paragraph) that means "unchanging." [1]
  10. Suggest a suitable title for this passage, other than one used above. [2]

Question 4 [20] — Summary & Note-Making

Read the passage below, then write a summary of it in not more than 80 words. Suggest a suitable title.

unseen passage
Handwriting, once considered an essential mark of a good education,
is quietly disappearing from daily life. Twenty years ago, a
student's notebook was full of pages written entirely by hand;
today, notes are typed into phones, tablets and laptops, often
before the ink of a physical pen has had the chance to dry on
anything at all. Teachers in several countries have noticed that
many students can no longer write a full page legibly without their
hand cramping, simply because they rarely practice the skill outside
of examination halls.

Researchers who study the connection between handwriting and memory
suggest that something important may be lost in this shift. Writing
by hand is slower than typing, and that very slowness appears to
help the brain process and retain information more deeply. When a
student types a lecture verbatim, she is often transcribing without
truly listening; when she writes by hand, the effort of choosing
what to include forces her to summarize and understand the material
as she goes.

This does not mean typing has no advantages -- it is faster, easier
to edit, and essential for the kind of professional communication
most students will do as adults. But educators increasingly argue
for a balance: encouraging handwritten notes during lectures, for
instance, while allowing typed work for longer essays and
assignments, so that students keep both skills rather than losing
one entirely to the convenience of the other.

4. Marking Guide

✓

Question 1 — Composition: what an examiner checks

Content & development (10): Does the composition stay on topic, with ideas developed rather than merely listed? Organisation (6): Is there a clear beginning, middle and end, with logical paragraph breaks? Language & style (6): Is vocabulary varied and appropriate, with sentence structures that aren't all the same length and shape? Grammatical accuracy (3): Are tense, agreement and punctuation mostly correct? A composition significantly under or over 500 words typically loses marks under content/organisation for being under-developed or unfocused, respectively.

✓

Question 2 — Formal Email: what an examiner checks

Format (2): Subject line, appropriate salutation (e.g. "Dear Sir/Madam" or "Respected Principal"), and a formal complimentary close (e.g. "Yours sincerely"). Content (5): For (a), the request, reason, proposed date and expected benefits should all be present; for (b), the problem and at least two distinct, practical solutions. Language & tone (3): Formal register throughout — no contractions, no casual phrasing, concise sentences within the word limit.

✓

Question 3 — Comprehension (full answers)

(a) He studied violinists, chess players and surgeons, over a period of more than thirty years.

(b) Not natural ability or talent, but the amount of deliberate practice each person had accumulated.

(c) Because ordinary, repeated driving does not push him beyond what he already finds comfortable — it lacks the deliberate targeting of weaknesses that true deliberate practice requires.

(d) It requires attempting something slightly beyond one's current ability, receiving immediate feedback on what went wrong, and trying again while incorporating that feedback.

(e) It illustrates that isolating and repeatedly correcting a specific weakness (the four difficult bars) produces faster improvement than repeating an entire piece unchanged from start to finish.

(f) The amount of time spent in focused, deliberate practice, and a willingness to treat mistakes as useful information rather than as proof of a fixed limitation — not any innate advantage.

(g) No. Supporting phrase: "a good coach, a well-structured curriculum and honest feedback all matter enormously."

(h) Talent.

(i) Fixed.

(j) Any suitable title reflecting the passage's central idea, e.g. "The Myth of Natural Talent" or "What Really Separates Experts from Amateurs."

✓

Question 4 — Summary (model answer)

Model summary (78 words): "Handwriting is declining as digital devices replace notebooks, leaving many students unable to write legibly for long. Research suggests handwriting's slowness helps the brain process and retain information better than typing, which often becomes mere transcription. However, typing remains faster and essential for professional communication. Educators therefore recommend balancing both skills — using handwriting for lecture notes and typing for longer written work — so students retain the cognitive benefits of writing by hand without losing typing's practical advantages."

Suitable title: "The Decline of Handwriting" or "Handwriting vs. Typing in the Digital Age."

Marking breakdown: Content/key points covered (12) — decline of handwriting, the memory/retention research finding, typing's advantages, the recommended balance; Language & expression (4) — summary is in the student's own words, not copied phrases strung together; Title (2); staying within the word limit (2).
